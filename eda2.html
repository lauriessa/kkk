VISUALIZATION
To help visualize and better understand the relationship between the variables, a scatter plot was used to display the association between poverty incidence and food price volatility across regions. Each point in the graph represents a region, positioned according to its corresponding poverty level (x-axis) and volatility measure (y-axis).

The scatter plot ABOVE shows a downward-sloping trendline, indicating a negative relationship between the two variables. The data points follow a relatively consistent downward pattern, implying that the relationship is not random. While some variation exists across regions, the overall distribution supports the presence of an inverse association between poverty and volatility. Furthermore, the pattern appears approximately linear, as the points cluster around the fitted line without significant curvature or directional shifts.
HYPOTHESIS TESTING
As the data consists of cross-sectional regional observations, we analyzed the relationship between food price volatility and poverty incidence using both Pearson and Spearman correlation tests. These tests are appropriate for determining whether a statistically significant association exists between two variables.

The Pearson correlation test measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship, while the Spearman correlation test assesses a monotonic relationship, which does not require linearity and is more robust to non-normal data and outliers. Using both methods ensures that the results are consistent and reliable.

This approach aligns with the objective of the study: to determine whether variations in food price volatility are associated with differences in poverty levels across regions.

For both tests, the hypotheses are defined as follows:

  • Null Hypothesis: There is no relationship between food price volatility and poverty incidence.
  • Alternative Hypothesis: There is a relationship between food price volatility and poverty incidence.

A significance level of α = 0.05 was used. When the p-value is less than 0.05, the null hypothesis is rejected, indicating a statistically significant relationship.

Additionally, both correlation coefficients are negative (r = -0.7320; ρ = -0.7304), indicating that the relationship is inverse in direction.
DISCUSSION
The scatter plot and the results of the Pearson and Spearman correlation tests indicate a statistically significant inverse relationship between regional food price volatility from 2021 to 2025 and poverty incidence across regions. The downward-sloping trendline in the graph suggests that regions with higher poverty incidence generally experienced lower levels of food price volatility, while regions with lower poverty incidence tended to experience greater fluctuations in food prices. The relatively consistent distribution of the data points further supports the presence of this negative association.

The statistical analyses reinforce this observation. The Pearson correlation coefficient of −0.7320 indicates a strong negative linear relationship between the two variables, while the Spearman correlation coefficient of −0.7304 confirms that the inverse relationship also holds when the data are ranked. Since the p-values for both tests (Pearson: p = 0.0008; Spearman: p = 0.0009) are lower than the 0.05 significance level, the null hypothesis of no significant relationship is rejected.

These findings imply that poorer regions may experience more stable food prices compared to wealthier regions, which tend to show greater price volatility. One possible explanation is that poorer regions often have lower market activity, limited consumer demand, or stronger dependence on locally produced staple goods, resulting in smaller price fluctuations. In contrast, regions with lower poverty incidence may have more active and interconnected markets that are more sensitive to supply chain disruptions, changing consumer demand, and broader economic conditions. Overall, the consistency between the visual trend and the statistical results strengthens the reliability of the conclusion that higher poverty incidence is associated with lower food price volatility.
